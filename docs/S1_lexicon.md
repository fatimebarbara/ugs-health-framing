# S1.  Lexicon: construction, match counts and full multilingual list

*Supplementary material to: Hegyi, F.B. What Is a Park For? Measuring the Health Framing of Urban Green Space Across 22 European Capitals.*

This file complements Appendix A of the paper, which gives the matching rule and the 92 terms with their match counts (Tables A2 and A3). It adds how the lexicon was built, what the match counts show, the language searched for each account, and every term's variants in all 22 languages.

## S1.1  How the lexicon was built

The lexicon was built in seven steps.

1. Starting point: the SDG vocabulary. The controlled vocabulary of Duran-Silva and colleagues, version 1.2, was searched for every term that denotes a green space or its elements (under Goals 11 and 15) and every term that names a health outcome (under Goal 3). Twenty-six terms were carried over, with their existing translations, and are marked SDG v1.2 in the tables below: 17 in Layer A and 9 in Layer B. They include green space, green area, green infrastructure, public parks, forest, playground, public space, well-being, quality of life, mental health, air quality and air pollution. These are the terms a standard SDG analysis already has.

2. Structure: two layers and a code. The terms were split into Layer A, which names a physical green space, and Layer B, which names a pathway from green space to health. Layer B was organised around the three mechanisms of Markevych and colleagues — reducing harm, restoring capacities, and building capacities — plus two codes that are not mechanisms: outcome, for terms that name health directly, and beneficiary, for terms that name who is served. The code is what distinguishes a post that says a park is healthy from a post that says a park is where children play.

3. Extension from the posts. A stratified pilot of 105 posts across all 23 cities was read before the lexicon was fixed, and the terms that carried green space and health in those posts but were absent from the SDG vocabulary were added: park and tree as bare nouns, square, promenade, greenway, walking, cycling, shade, green lung, community, children. Sixty-six terms were added this way, 27 in Layer A and 39 in Layer B, and are marked new. The pilot showed that the SDG vocabulary's multi-word policy phrases — urban public spaces, green cover index — almost never occur in a mayor's post, while the bare nouns occur constantly.

4. Per-language compilation. For each of the 22 languages the terms were compiled against municipal usage rather than translated. Where a city has a word of its own for the thing, that word was listed: the Roman villa for the city's major parks, the Catalan superilla and refugi climàtic, the Viennese Coole Straße, the Madrid Bosque Metropolitano, the Lisbon corredor verde, the Parisian îlot de fraîcheur and square, the Warsaw łąka kwietna, the Sofia ozelenyavane and gradinka. Where a language inflects, the common inflected forms were listed as separate variants (parkas, parko, parke, parkai, parkų in Lithuanian), because matching is on the exact variant and allows no suffix (Appendix A). For languages the author does not read, candidate terms were generated with the assistance of a large language model and checked against the posts themselves before inclusion, so that each listed variant is a form that occurs in municipal usage in that language rather than a dictionary translation.

5. Collisions. Terms that matched in an unintended sense were not removed from the lexicon but logged, so that their effect could be measured rather than hidden. The seven collision classes and the per-language exclusion stems are in S2.

6. Versions. Version 2 is the version used for the census and for all figures in the paper. It was in place before the census began on 17 August 2026. The only later change to the working file was the addition, on 21 September 2026, of the column holding the five codes; that change added no term and removed none.

7. Correction before deposit. In preparing the deposit, ten consecutive Layer B rows — green lung, shade, cooling, cool island, heat, urban heat island, heatwave, air quality, air pollution and clean air — were found to carry the non-English variants of the neighbouring row: the English column was correct, but in the 21 other language columns each term held the variants of the next term in the list, and clean air held those of green lung. The deposited file (lexicon_v2.1) corrects this. The correction does not change T1, which uses only outcome terms, or T2, which uses all 48 Layer B terms together; it changes only which code a match is counted under. Among the 185 health-framed posts, harm terms now appear in 11 rather than 12; the other code counts are unchanged.

## S1.2  What the match counts in Tables A2 and A3 show

In Layer A (Table A2), two things stand out. The bare nouns do the work: park alone matched 403 of the 779 valid posts, tree 67, green space 93; the SDG vocabulary's compound terms — green cover index, urban public spaces, native tree — matched none, and 13 of the 44 terms matched no valid post at all. And the retrieved and valid columns are nearly equal for every Layer A term (park 415 and 403, square 45 and 43), which confirms that the 741 rejections came from the retrieval stems that are not in this table — bare green, grün, verde and their collisions — and not from these terms. Of the 779 valid posts, 590 contain at least one Layer A term; 157 contain one of the 17 terms taken from the SDG vocabulary, 512 one of the 27 new terms, and 433 only new terms. Of the 185 health-framed posts, 49 contain an SDG-derived Layer A term.

In Layer B (Table A3), the physical activity terms carry T2: walking and cycling matched 30 and 28 valid posts and were health-framed in 19 and 17 of them. The outcome terms are precise and rare: health itself matched 25 valid posts, 23 of them health-framed, which is why T1 has high precision and low recall. The beneficiary terms are imprecise — children 7 of 16, families 3 of 14 — which is the finding behind the T2-without-beneficiary variant; so is community, a build term, at 8 of 24. Mental restoration is the least precise term in the list (1 of 9), because its Spanish and Catalan variants recuperación and recuperació also mean economic recovery. Fifteen of the 48 Layer B terms never matched a valid post; they are kept in the deposited lexicon because their absence is itself informative about how mayors write.
